    "## 0 | Linear Transformations\n",
    "\n",
    "Linear algebra at its essence is about linear transformations on vectors.\n",
    "\n",
    "A transformation $A$ on a vector $\\vec{v}$ which transforms it to a vector $\\vec{v'}$ ($A \\vec{v} = \\vec{v'}$) is linear if:\n",
    "\n",
    "1. For arbitrary vectors $\\vec{v}, \\vec{w}$: $A(\\vec{v} + \\vec{w}) = A\\vec{v} + A \\vec{w}$\n",
    "2. For arbitrary vector $\\vec{v}$ and constant $c$: $A(c*\\vec{v}) = c*A\\vec{v}$\n",
    "\n",
    "Matrices equipped with the matrix multiplication via the way it was explained in the previous linear algebra section satisfy these two properties, and in fact any linear transformation can be written as a matrix! That is why linear algebra is often presented in the form of matrices.\n",
    "\n",
    "Examples of linear transformations include:\n",
    " - rotation, reflection, shear, projections and scaling of vectors in space (everything except the last row here)\n",
    "![Linear transformations](https://mathigon.org/content/linear-algebra/images/transformations.svg)\n",
    " - differentiation on the vector space of polynomial functions \n",
    " - differentiation on the vector space of exponential functions (really important in differential equations, as we will see later)\n",
    "\n",
    "\n",
    "### 0.1 Writing down the matrix for a linear transformation\n",
    "\n",
    "As I said above, any linear transformation can be written as a matrix. Let's say we want to rotate a vector 30 degrees clockwise. This might be relevant if we have currents measured in an along track/cross track basis and we want to compare them to a dataset that has north-south/east-west currents. One thing we could do is transform the north-south/east-west currents to along track/cross track. We are going to do a simpler example, where we just rotate a vector by 45 degrees clockwise from its original position, but this could be extended to use for along/cross track transformation.\n",
    "\n",
    "![swot velocities](../_static/swot_velocities.png)\n",
    "\n",
    "To do this, we need to first see what the rotation transformation does to the \"north\" and \"east\" unit vectors.\n",
    "\n",
    "In the case of a 45° clockwise rotation, a \"north\" vector becomes evenly split between north and east. We know that its length is still 1, so we can say that $$R \\hat{n} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} (\\hat{n} + \\hat{e})$$, where $R$ is the rotation transformation. Similarly, $$R \\hat{e} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} (-\\hat{n} + \\hat{e})$$\n",
    "\n",
    "Now, if we write our velocities as a sum of velocities in the east and north directions: $$\\vec{v} = v \\hat{n} + u \\hat{e},$$ \n",
    "\n",
    "It is relatively simple to write what a rotation looks like, using the expresssions for what the rotation does to the unit vectors. A bit of algebra will tell us that $$R \\vec{v} = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}} [(v - u)\\hat{n} + (v + u)\\hat{e}].$$ We can therefore write this transformation as a matrix like this: $$R = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2}}\\begin{pmatrix}\n",
    "1 & -1\\\\\n",
    "1 & 1 \n",
    "\\end{pmatrix}$$\n",
    "\n",
    "Some exercises from Tommy's old notes:\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "**Question**\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "If we wanted to rotate our coordinate system counter-clockwise by 90 degrees, what would be the transformation matrix?\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "**Solution**\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "$$\n",
    "A' = \\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}\n",
    "$$\n",
    "\n",

    "\n",
    "$$\n",
    "A(\\theta)' = \\begin{bmatrix} f(\\theta) & g(\\theta) \\\\ h(\\theta) & l(\\theta) \\end{bmatrix}\n",
    "$$\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "Solution\n",
    "\n",
    "---\n",
    "\n",
    "$$\n",
    "A(\\theta)' = \\begin{bmatrix} \\cos(\\theta) & -\\sin(\\theta) \\\\ \\sin(\\theta) & \\cos(\\theta) \\end{bmatrix}\n",
    "$$"

    "## 2 | Definitions \\& finding eigenvectors\n",
    "\n",
    "Let's say we have a vector $\\vec{x}$ which satisfies the following criterion:\n",
    "$$\n",
    "\\begin{equation}\n",
    "A \\vec{x} = \\lambda \\vec{x}\n",
    "\\end{equation}\n",
    "$$\n",
    "\n",
    "for some number $\\lambda$. In words, this means that the transformation $A$ simply scales $\\vec{x}$ by a scalar multiple, which is unusual! If the above is true, $\\vec{x}$ is an eigenvector of the transformation $A$ and $\\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. One thing to emphasize here is that eigenvectors and their corresponding eigenvalues are associated with particular transformations. \n",
    "\n",
    "How do we find the eigenvectors of a transformation? If we can represent the transformation as a matrix (this is true for all finite-dimension transformations), there is a formulaic way to do it. To start, let's introduce the identity matrix:\n",
    "\\begin{equation}\n",
    "I =\n",
    "\\begin{pmatrix}\n",
    "1 & 0 & 0 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "0 & 1 & 0 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "0 & 0 & 1 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "\\vdots & \\vdots & \\vdots & \\ddots & \\vdots \\\\\n",
    "0 & 0 & 0 & \\cdots & 1\n",
    "\\end{pmatrix}.\n",
    "\\end{equation}\n",
    "We can write Eq. 1 as\n",
    "\\begin{equation*}\n",
    "A \\vec{x} = (\\lambda I) \\vec{x}\n",
    "\\end{equation*}\n",
    "and thus \n",
    "\\begin{equation*}\n",
    "(A-\\lambda I) \\vec{x} = \\vec{0}.\n",
    "\\end{equation*}\n",
    "\n",
    "For the above to be true for nonzero $\\vec{x},$ $(A-\\lambda I)$ must be a noninvertible matrix (Otherwise $(A-\\lambda I)^{-1} \\vec{0}= \\vec{x}$, which is impossible). This means that $\\det (A-\\lambda I)=0.$ This means that we can find the eigenvalues for a matrix $A$ by solving the equation $\\det (A-\\lambda I)=0.$, which will be a polynomial equation. \n",
    "\n",
    "To get the eigenvector for an eigenvalue $\\lambda_i$, we return to the equation \n",
    "\\begin{equation*}\n",
    "(A-\\lambda I) \\vec{x} = \\vec{0}.\n",
    "\\end{equation*}\n",
    "and plug in $\\lambda_i$ for lambda. Setting each element of $x$ to an arbitrary value $x_i$, we have a linear system of equations which can be solved to back out $x_i$. A scalar multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector of the same value, so it is often conventional to normalize the eigenvector such that the sum of the squares of each element is 1 ($\\sum_i{x_i^2}=1$). This is easier to see in practice, and we will use the matrix\n",
    "\\begin{equation*}\n",
    "A=\\begin{pmatrix}\n",
    "4 & 3\\\\\n",
    "3 & -4 \n",
    "\\end{pmatrix}\n",
    "\\end{equation*}\n",
    "as our example.\n",

    "## 3 | Diagonalization\n",
    "\n",
    "What was this all for? Eigenvalues and eigenvectors have a few uses, but for me an essential use case is making computations that would be otherwise hard/impossible into computations that are easier/doable using diagonalization. Diagonalization is the process of writing a linear transformation in the basis of its own eigenvalues. The reason it is called diagonalization is that a matrix written in the basis of its own eigenvectors $\\{\\lambda_i\\}$ is diagonal:\n",
    "\n",
    "\\begin{equation*}\n",
    "\\Lambda =\n",
    "\\begin{pmatrix}\n",
    "\\lambda_1 & 0 & 0 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "0 & \\lambda_2 & 0 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "0 & 0 & \\lambda_3 & \\cdots & 0 \\\\\n",
    "\\vdots & \\vdots & \\vdots & \\ddots & \\vdots \\\\\n",
    "0 & 0 & 0 & \\cdots & \\lambda_n\n",
    "\\end{pmatrix}.\n",
    "\\end{equation*}\n",
    "\n",
    "If you have a vector written in the same basis, applying the linear transformation to that vector is trivial. The process of diagonalization gets us there.\n",
    "\n",
    "### 3.1 Performing a diagonalization\n",
    "To perform a diagonalization on a finite-dimensional linear transformation $A$, we first need to find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$. We then construct the diagonalization matrix $S$ by making its columns the (normalized) eigenvectors of $A$. \n",
    "\\begin{equation}\n",
    "S =\n",
    "\\begin{pmatrix}\n",
    "| & | &  & | \\\\\n",
    "v_1 & v_2 & \\cdots & v_n \\\\\n",
    "| & | &  & |\n",
    "\\end{pmatrix}\n",
    "\\end{equation}\n",
    "\n",
    "We already can find the diagonal matrix $\\Lambda$ by writing the eigenvalues on the diagonal, but it is worth noting that $\\Lambda = S^{-1} A S.$ To write a vector $\\vec{v}$ in the diagonal basis, you multiply it by $S$:\n",
    "\n",
    "\\begin{equation}\n",
    "\\vec{v}_{\\text{diag}}= S^{-1} \\vec{v}.\n",
    "\\end{equation}\n",
